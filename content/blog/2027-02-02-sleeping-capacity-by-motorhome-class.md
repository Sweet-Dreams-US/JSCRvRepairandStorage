When you shop for a motorhome, one of the first numbers on the listing is how many people it sleeps. It is a useful number, but it can also mislead. A motorhome that sleeps six on paper may feel crowded with six real people in it. This post compares what industry sources say about Class A, Class B, and Class C motorhomes, and shows how to read the number.

## Class A motorhomes

Go RVing says Class A motorhomes sleep up to 8 people.

Another industry source, the RV Dealers Association, gives a range of two to seven people for Class A. The two sources are close, but they do not match. That is common, and it is the first reason to treat the advertised number as an upper limit and not a promise.

## Class B motorhomes

Class B motorhomes are the smallest of the three, at 16 to 21 feet according to Go RVing Canada. Go RVing says they sleep 2 to 4 people. Go RVing Canada says a Class B sleeps up to 4 people comfortably.

The RV Dealers Association is more cautious. It says Class B motorhomes usually seat and sleep two people, but some designs accommodate four.

Beds in a Class B come in several forms. Go RVing Canada notes that some have a couch that converts into a bed, and others use a Murphy-type bed, a bunkhouse, a standard bed, or a loft that lowers into place for sleeping.

## Class C motorhomes

Class C motorhomes are built on a van frame with a wider body attached to the original cab. Go RVing lists them at 25 to 35 feet, and lists the capacity as up to 8 people, with its feature section saying they sleep 6 to 8.

The over-the-cab area is often an optional sleeping area, and some models have a loft bed above the cockpit. Others have a separate bedroom with a king or queen size bed. Go RVing also notes that slide-outs can expand the living, dining, and kitchen area.

The RV Dealers Association gives a lower range for Class C: two to six people, if you include children.

:::note Worth knowing
Go RVing lists sleeping capacity as up to 8 for both Class A and Class C motorhomes, and 2 to 4 for Class B. The RV Dealers Association lists two to seven for Class A, usually two for Class B, and two to six for Class C.
:::

## Why the numbers differ

The ranges differ for a few reasons. Floor plans within a class vary a lot, and Go RVing Canada describes a wide array of floorplans, sizes, and features. The RV Dealers Association's Class C figure explicitly includes children, so counts may assume different mixes of adults and children.

The RV Dealers Association gives two pieces of advice on this. It tells renters to be careful of small units that claim to sleep five, six, or more. It also reminds readers that adults need a full 6 foot length for comfort. A bed that counts toward the total may not work for an adult.

## Sleeping capacity and weight

There is a second reason to treat the number carefully. Sleeping capacity also affects how much weight you can carry.

The RV writer Andy Herrick explains in his guide to RV weight ratings that cargo carrying capacity is the weight left over for your belongings after other weights are subtracted. He says it is a formal term found on the tire sticker of your RV. One of the items subtracted is the sleeping capacity weight rating, which he gives as 154 pounds for each sleeping place.

:::note Cargo capacity
Herrick explains that the sleeping capacity weight rating is subtracted from the vehicle's gross weight rating, along with the empty weight, full fresh water, full water heater, and the propane in full onboard tanks. What is left is the cargo carrying capacity.
:::

In practice, a motorhome with more sleeping places has a share of its weight capacity set aside for people before you pack a single bag. Look at the tire sticker before you decide how many people you plan to carry and how much gear they will bring.

## How to choose

A few steps will help you compare listings.

Count your real group, not your best case. If you travel with two adults and two children, a unit that sleeps eight is not necessarily better than one that sleeps four. It may only be bigger.

Look at the beds themselves. Check the sizes, how each one is made up, and how much work it takes each night.

Think about privacy. The RV Dealers Association advises paying attention to how much privacy you will have when sleeping.

Ask what you can see for yourself. Walk through the unit with your whole group in it, and lie down on the beds if the seller allows it.

## The short version

Class A and Class C motorhomes are listed at up to 8 sleepers, and Class B at 2 to 4, but other industry sources give lower numbers for the same classes. Treat the listed figure as the most a floor plan can hold, check the beds, and check the weight capacity on the tire sticker before you plan the trip.
