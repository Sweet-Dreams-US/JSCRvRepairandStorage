A Class B motorhome is often called a camper van. If you are weighing one against a bigger rig, the first question is simple: what actually fits inside?

This article goes through the rooms and features of a Class B one at a time, then compares them with a Class C so you can see where the space goes. The facts come from the Go RVing pages listed at the end.

## The size of a Class B

Go RVing describes Class B motorhomes as having all the conveniences of a furnished motor home, but being compact enough that driving one is as easy as driving your regular van or SUV. The page lists "easy parking" and "nimble and easy to drive" among the features.

The page does not give dimensions, so this article does not either. What matters is the idea behind the design: a Class B uses its space efficiently, because there is not much of it. Every area has to do more than one job.

:::note Worth knowing
Go RVing says Class B RVs offer a kitchen, seating, a bathroom and a place to sleep, all inside a compact body.
:::

## The kitchen

A Class B has a compact kitchen. That means a small space for preparing and cooking meals, sized for a camper van and not for a house. Plan your cooking around that. Simple meals and a short list of tools work better than a full set of cookware.

If you are used to a residential kitchen, this is the biggest change. A Class C, by comparison, comes with a residential kitchen with full-size appliances as standard equipment, according to Go RVing. The kitchen is one of the clearest differences between the two classes.

## The bathroom

Go RVing lists simple bathroom facilities for Class B motorhomes, and also says that many have a full wet or dry bath. In other words, the bathroom varies from one model to the next. Some are basic, and some are full baths.

When you shop, look closely at the bathroom in each van you consider. Check how it works, how much room you have to move, and whether it suits the way you travel. A Class C, by contrast, is described as having a full-size dry bath with a toilet, sink and shower or bath.

## Seating and sleeping

Go RVing says a Class B sleeps 2 to 4 people. It also lists seating as one of the four main features, along with the kitchen, bathroom and sleeping area. In a van this small, seating and sleeping often share the same space, which is part of what "efficient" means here.

A Class C sleeps more. Go RVing lists capacity for up to 8 people, and says a Class C is large enough for a separate bedroom with a king or queen bed, closet space and storage. It also notes that the area over the cab is often an optional sleeping area, usually used as a loft bed.

## How a Class C compares

Go RVing gives Class C motorhomes a length of 25 to 35 feet. They are built on an automotive van frame with a wider body attached to the original cab. They can have slide-outs that enlarge the living area, with some models moving a wall out up to three feet. They also offer a shade awning, space for a home office, ample storage, an entertainment system, and the ability to tow a small vehicle for side trips.

That is a lot more room, and it is the main reason people choose a Class C. The tradeoff is the one the Class B page points to: a Class B is easier to drive and easier to park.

## Which one fits your trips

A short checklist can help you decide.

First, count the people. A Class B sleeps 2 to 4, while a Class C is listed for up to 8. Second, think about cooking. If you want full-size appliances, a Class C is the one Go RVing describes that way. Third, think about where you drive and park. If easy parking and a van-like drive matter most, the Class B description fits.

None of this replaces walking through a rig in person. Sit at the table, lie on the bed, and stand in the bath space before you decide. Bring the people who will travel with you, and ask yourself whether each of them has a place to sit, a place to sleep, and room to move. A floor plan on paper cannot answer those questions the way a walk-through can.

## After you buy

Small spaces put more weight on every system. A leaking line, a failed appliance or a tired battery affects the whole rig, because there is nowhere else to go. That is a good reason to set up regular maintenance early, before the first long trip.
