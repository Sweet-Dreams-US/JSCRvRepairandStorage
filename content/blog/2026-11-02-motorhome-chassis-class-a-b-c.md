If you own a motorhome, you have heard the words Class A, Class B and Class C. They sound like grades, as if one class is better than another. They are not grades. They are a way of sorting motorhomes by how they are built and how much room they give you. Knowing which class you own makes it easier to read a manual, to shop for parts, and to describe a problem to a service shop.

This post explains how the three classes differ, starting with the part that sets them apart most: the chassis.

## Why motorhomes are sorted into classes

A motorhome is an RV with the engine and driving seat built in. You do not hitch it to anything, which separates it from a travel trailer or a fifth wheel. Go RVing sorts motorhomes into three groups: Class A, Class B and Class C.

The groups are not about price or age. They describe the shape of the vehicle, the frame it is built on, and the living space inside. Two motorhomes in the same class can still be very different, so think of the class as a starting point and not a full description.

## Class A: a chassis made for the job

Go RVing says a Class A motorhome is built on a "specially designed motor vehicle chassis," so no separate tow vehicle is needed.

Inside, Class A coaches have large living and entertainment areas, a full kitchen, and a full-sized bathroom and toilet. The private bedroom is separate from the living space, and some models have large vanities and double sinks. Go RVing lists sleeping for up to eight people, plenty of storage, and the ability to tow another vehicle for side trips once you park.

The cockpit is where new owners notice the difference. Go RVing says a Class A cockpit has more screens and controls than many vehicles, and that backup cameras and alert systems help new drivers adjust.

## Class B: the camper van

Class B motorhomes are camper vans. Go RVing describes them as "compact enough to make driving one as easy as your regular van or SUV." Everything is smaller: a compact kitchen, seating, a bathroom and a sleeping area, all in a layout that fits a van body.

Go RVing lists sleeping for two to four people. The bathroom facilities are simple, and some models have a full wet or dry bath while others do not. The tradeoff is clear. You give up space and gain easy parking and nimble handling.

## Class C: a van frame with a wider body

Go RVing describes a Class C as "built on an automotive van frame with a wider body section attached to the original cab." That sentence is the quickest way to tell a Class C from the others. If you look at the front of the vehicle and see a van-style cab, with a wider living section built behind it, you are likely looking at a Class C.

The part of the body that reaches over the cab is often an optional sleeping area. Go RVing says Class C owners use the space above the cockpit as a loft bed. Class C motorhomes can have a full-sized kitchen and bathroom, and Go RVing says they are large enough to include a separate bedroom with a king or queen size bed. Slide-outs on some models extend the living space by up to three feet.

Go RVing's Class C page gives a length range of 25 to 35 feet and sleeping for up to eight people. Like a Class A, a Class C can tow a small vehicle for short trips once the motorhome is parked.

:::note Worth knowing
Go RVing says that for most RVs, a regular driver's license is all that is needed, though this varies by state. A small number of states require extra steps, such as a skills or written test, for very large motorhomes. Check your own state's rules before you buy or drive a large coach.
:::

## Sleeping numbers are a ceiling, not a promise

Go RVing lists "up to eight" for Class A and Class C and two to four for Class B. The words "up to" matter. A floor plan with a bunk, a sofa that folds out and a dinette that converts can reach that number on paper, but you may not use that many beds in practice. When you compare classes, count the beds you would really use and the storage you would really need, not the maximum.

The same habit helps when you plan a trip. A larger coach gives more room to move around, and a smaller one gives more choice about where to park. Neither is better in general. The right class is the one that fits how you camp.

## What this means when something needs repair

Most repair conversations begin with where the problem is. Is it in the cab, in the living area, or underneath? The class tells a shop a lot about what it is looking at. A Class C has a cab section and a wider body behind it. A Class A has a coach built on its own chassis. A Class B is a van body with everything packed into it.

Be specific about what you notice. Instead of saying the slide-out is broken, say whether it stops partway, makes a noise, or will not move at all. Instead of saying the water does not work, say which faucet, and whether the pump runs. A clear description shortens the first call and helps the shop plan the visit.

Keep your manuals and any paperwork from the dealer. Those documents often list the exact model and chassis details, and they are the quickest way to answer questions about parts.

## A short way to remember the classes

Class A: built on a chassis designed for a motorhome, with large living areas. Class B: a camper van, compact and easy to drive and park. Class C: a van frame with a wider body attached to the original cab, often with a loft over the cab. If you remember those three ideas, you can sort most motorhomes you see.

If you are still not sure which class you own, look at the cab and the front of the vehicle first, then check your manual or ask the dealer who sold it. When you know the class, the rest of the conversation about your RV gets easier.
