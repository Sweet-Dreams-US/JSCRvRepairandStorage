Go RVing describes Class A motorhomes as built with the comforts of home in mind. That is a good way to think about the inside of one. If you are deciding between RV types, the interior is where the differences show up most. This post walks through what a Class A interior usually includes, room by room, and what to look at once you are standing inside one.

A Class A is built on a motor vehicle chassis. That means you do not need a separate tow vehicle, and the driver sits in a cockpit at the front rather than in a pickup or SUV. Everything behind that cockpit is living space, and the layout is what makes the interior feel closer to a small house than to a camper.

## The living area

The living area is the first thing most people notice. Go RVing says a Class A has large living and entertainment areas that feel like a house, with comfortable furniture and larger cabinets and sinks than you would find in smaller RV types.

For daily use, this matters more than it sounds. On a rainy day at a campground, the living area is where everyone ends up. A bigger space means one person can cook, another can sit with a book, and a third can watch something, without everyone being in the same two feet of floor.

When you walk through a living area, sit in the seats. Check that the furniture is comfortable for the length of time you plan to use it, not only for a five minute tour. Open the cabinets and look at how much they hold. Notice where the walkway runs, because a layout that looks open when empty can feel tight once it holds your gear.

## The kitchen

Go RVing lists a full kitchen as a standard part of the Class A interior. In practice that means room for cooking real meals instead of reheating food.

Look at counter space first. A full kitchen is only useful if there is somewhere to set a cutting board and a pot at the same time. Then look at the appliances, how the cabinets close, and whether drawers stay shut while the RV is moving. Ask how the appliances run, since an RV kitchen can draw on more than one power source.

Appliances are one of the systems that need attention over time. Routine checks before each season catch small problems before they turn into a failed refrigerator on a trip.

## The bathroom

A Class A has a full-sized bathroom and toilet, according to Go RVing, and some larger models add large vanities and double sinks. This is a real difference from compact RV types, where the bathroom can be tight.

When you inspect a bathroom, run the water. Check the faucet, the shower, and the toilet. Look at the floor and the walls around the shower for any sign of moisture or soft spots, since water damage is easier to deal with early than late. A bathroom can look perfect and still have a slow leak behind a panel, so look and smell as well as touch.

## A separate bedroom

Go RVing points out that the size and layout of a Class A make a private bedroom, separate from the living space, possible. For many travelers this is the deciding feature. A door between the bedroom and the living area means one person can sleep while another stays up.

Check the bed itself. Lie down on it. Confirm that you can get in and out without climbing over a partner, and that there is a place for clothes and a place to set a phone or glasses. If the bedroom has a slide-out, extend it and retract it a few times and watch how it moves.

For comparison, Go RVing describes the Class C layout differently. In a Class C the space above the cab is used as a loft bed, and the page says that area is often an optional sleeping area. Class C models can also include a separate bedroom with a king or queen size bed. Both types can sleep up to eight people, so the difference is in how the space is arranged, not only in how many people it holds.

## Storage and the rest of the layout

Go RVing lists ample storage as a feature of a Class A. Interior storage includes cabinets and closets, and a Class A can also tow another vehicle for side trips, which changes how you think about what you carry inside.

When you tour one, open every cabinet and every closet. Think about your own trip. If you travel with a lot of cooking gear, look at the kitchen cabinets. If you travel for months, look at the closet and the space for shoes and coats. Storage you will not use is not a feature for you, and storage you need and cannot find is a problem you will notice on the first day.

## What to check inside before you buy or travel

The interior is a collection of systems, and each one can wear out. Before you buy a used Class A or head out for a season, go through the inside in order.

First, plumbing. Run every faucet, flush the toilet, and look under sinks for drips. Second, electrical. Turn on every light, test outlets, and check that the switches do what they say. Third, appliances. Run the refrigerator, stove, and any heating and cooling units. Fourth, slide-outs. Extend and retract each one, and listen for grinding or hesitation. Fifth, ceilings and walls. Look for stains, discoloration, or soft spots, which can point to a roof or seal problem.

None of this needs special tools. It needs time and attention, and a written list so you do not skip a step. If you find something you are not sure about, that is a good moment to have someone who works on RVs look at it.

## Putting it together

A Class A interior is built around a large living area, a full kitchen, a full-sized bathroom, and a separate bedroom. Those four spaces are the reason people describe these motorhomes as feeling like a house. The same spaces are also where plumbing, electrical, appliances, and slide-outs all live, so they are where regular care pays off.

Use the room by room checks in this post when you tour one, and bring a list. Sit in the seats, run the water, test the lights, and move the slide-outs. If you plan to travel with it, think about how you will actually use each room. The best interior is the one that fits the way you travel.
