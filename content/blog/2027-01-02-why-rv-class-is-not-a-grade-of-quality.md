When people shop for a motorhome, they often hear the classes described like school grades. Class A sounds like the top of the list, and Class C sounds like a step down. That is a natural way to read the letters, but it is not what they mean. The classes are a way to sort motorhomes by how they are built and how big they are. They say nothing about how well a particular RV was made or how well it has been kept up.

This post explains what each class actually describes, why the letters do not rank quality, and what to look at when you want to judge one RV against another.

## What the classes are

Go RVing groups RVs into two broad categories. Towable RVs are pulled behind another vehicle. Drivable RVs, also called motorized RVs, have their own engine and do not need a separate tow vehicle. The three motorhome classes sit inside the drivable group.

The classes are sorted by the vehicle underneath the living space. That is the whole idea. Class A, B and C are three different starting points for a motorhome, and each one has a different shape, size and layout as a result.

## Class A: built on a motor vehicle chassis

Go RVing says Class A motorhomes are "built on specially designed motor vehicle chassis." The site lists a full kitchen and bathroom, sleeping for up to 8 people, and the ability to tow another vehicle for side trips.

Notice what that description covers. It covers the frame, the size and the layout. It does not say the interior is better finished, the roof is better sealed or the plumbing is more reliable. A Class A is a different kind of vehicle. It is not a higher grade of the same vehicle.

## Class B: the camper van

Class B motorhomes are camper vans. Go RVing describes them as "compact enough to make driving one as easy as your regular van or SUV." They sleep 2 to 4 people and have a compact kitchen and simpler bathroom facilities.

A Class B is smaller, so it holds fewer people and fewer features. That is a difference in size. For a couple who wants something easy to park and drive, the smaller size is the point, and it would be a mistake to call it a lower grade.

:::note Worth knowing
Go RVing describes Class B models as sleeping 2 to 4 people, and Class A and Class C models as sleeping up to 8.
:::

## Class C: built on a van frame

Go RVing says Class C motorhomes are "built on an automotive van frame with a wider body section attached to the original cab." The living area is wider than the cab, and the space above the cab is often used as a sleeping loft. The site lists a length of 25 to 35 feet, a full-size kitchen and bathroom, and sleeping for up to 8.

Go RVing describes them as a fit for families and groups of friends. The class is defined by the van frame and the wider body. Again, nothing in that definition is a quality rating.

## Why the letters do not rank quality

A class tells you the type of chassis, the rough size and the usual layout. Quality is about other things: how carefully the unit was assembled, what materials were used, how it has been stored, and how it has been maintained over the years. Two motorhomes of the same class can be in very different condition. A well-kept older unit of one class can be in better shape than a neglected newer unit of another.

The same is true for towables. Go RVing says towables range in size from 8 to 45 feet and account for 85% of the RV market. That is a very wide spread of sizes under one heading, and the label alone tells you little about any one trailer.

The practical point is that the class helps you narrow down what you need, such as how many people sleep aboard and how large a vehicle you are comfortable driving. It does not help you decide whether a specific RV is a good one.

## What to check instead

Once you know which type fits your trips, look at the individual unit. These are the places where condition shows up, whatever the class:

1. The roof and seals, and any sign of water getting into the unit.
2. Slide-outs, if the unit has them, and how smoothly they move in and out.
3. Awnings, and whether they roll out and retract cleanly.
4. Plumbing, including the pump, lines and any signs of past leaks.
5. Electrical systems and appliances, tested while the unit is plugged in.
6. Brakes and tires on anything that rolls.

A seller who can show maintenance records for these items is giving you real information. A letter on the brochure is not.

## Choosing by what you need

If you are deciding between classes, start with how you plan to use the RV. Think about how many people will travel, how large a vehicle you are willing to drive and park, and whether you want to tow something behind. Those questions point you toward a class far better than a belief that one letter is better than another.

Then, whatever you choose, plan for upkeep. Every class has a roof, plumbing, electrical systems and brakes, and every one of them needs regular attention. Seasonal preparation before storage and before the first trip of the year is part of owning any RV.

## The short version

Class A, B and C describe how a motorhome is built and how large it is. They do not describe how good it is. Use the class to find the type that suits your trips, then judge the individual RV by its condition and its maintenance history.
