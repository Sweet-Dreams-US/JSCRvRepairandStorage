When you call an RV shop about a problem, the first few minutes set the direction for everything after. The person on the phone is trying to picture your motorhome and your problem. The clearer the picture you give, the faster they can tell you what the visit will involve.

Starting with the class of your motorhome is one of the easiest ways to help. This post explains the three motorhome classes in plain terms, what else to say about the vehicle, and how to describe the problem itself.

## Why the class is the first thing to say

Motorhomes are grouped into Class A, Class B and Class C, and the groups differ in how they are built. A shop hears "Class C" or "Class A" and immediately knows what kind of body and chassis it is likely dealing with. That gives the conversation a starting point before you have said anything about the problem.

If you are not sure which class you own, you are not alone. Many owners bought the vehicle used, or simply never needed the term. The next section helps you work it out.

## The three motorhome classes in plain terms

Go RVing, the RV industry's consumer information site, describes the classes this way.

Class A motorhomes are the largest drivable option. They are built on specially designed motor vehicle chassis. They can sleep up to eight people and include a full kitchen, a full bathroom and a large living area.

Class B motorhomes are also called camper vans. They are the most compact option, close to a regular van in how they drive. They sleep two to four people and have a compact kitchen and basic bathroom facilities.

Class C motorhomes are built on an automotive van frame with a wider body attached to the original cab. The easiest way to recognize one is the portion over the cab, which is often an optional sleeping area. Go RVing says they can sleep up to eight people, include a full-sized kitchen and bathroom, and have slide-outs that expand the living area.

:::note How Go RVing describes a Class C
A Class C motorhome is built on an automotive van frame with a wider body attached to the original cab. It is easily recognizable by the over-the-cab portion, which is often an optional sleeping area. Go RVing gives a typical length of 25 to 35 feet.
:::

If your motorhome has a bed or storage area over the driver's cab, it is most likely a Class C. If it looks like a large bus with a flat front, it is most likely a Class A. If it looks like a van with a camper interior, it is a Class B. When in doubt, say what it looks like. A shop can work out the class from your description.

## Give the make, model and year

The class is a starting point, not the whole answer. Next say the make, model and year of the motorhome. Many motorhomes have two makers involved, one for the chassis and one for the living space, so give both if you know them. The registration, the insurance card and the paperwork from your purchase usually list them.

Having the vehicle identification number, called the VIN, ready is also a good idea. A shop may ask for it to look up parts or records. Take a photo of the number on your phone so you can read it out or send it without going back to the vehicle.

Mention the approximate mileage or engine hours if you know them, and whether the motorhome has slide-outs, an awning, a generator or a rooftop air conditioner. These details tell the shop which systems could be involved.

## Describe the problem, not only the part

Owners often name the part they think has failed. That can help, but the shop will still need to see the symptoms. Describe what you saw, heard or smelled, and when it happened.

Useful details include when the problem started, whether it happens all the time or only some of the time, and whether it comes with a sound, a smell, a warning light or a leak. If a slide-out stops halfway, say which one, and say whether it moves at all or makes noise. If water shows up inside, say where it appears and whether it follows rain or use of the plumbing.

Mention anything you have already tried, such as resetting a breaker or checking a fuse. That keeps the shop from repeating steps and shows what has been ruled out. Photos and short videos of the problem are worth sending if the shop accepts them.

## What to have ready before you call

A short list makes the call easier for both sides. Before you dial, have these on hand: the class, make, model and year; the VIN; the problem described in your own words; where the motorhome is right now; and when you could bring it in.

Also say whether the motorhome is drivable. A shop plans differently for a vehicle that can arrive under its own power than for one that has to be towed.

## A one-minute script

If you want a simple way to start the call, try this. "I have a Class C motorhome, a 2015 make and model. The slide-out in the living area stops partway when I open it. It started last weekend. I can drive it in." Swap in your own details. In about ten seconds, the shop knows the class, the vehicle, the problem, when it began and whether it can come in on its own.
